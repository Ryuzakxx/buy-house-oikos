1. Your brief and your real budget

Start by writing down what you want, and sorting it: what you can't do without (must haves), what would be a bonus (nice to haves) and what rules a home out (deal breakers). Our guided interview does exactly this.

Then the budget. On top of the price, plan for the purchase taxes (they depend on who sells and on whether the home will be your main residence), the notary's fee, the agency commission if there is one, surveys, translations and currency costs. The financing guide has a worksheet.

2. Codice fiscale and a bank account

You need a codice fiscale to sign an offer, so get it before your viewing trip. An Italian bank account isn't required for the purchase, but you'll want one for utilities and local taxes, and it's required if you take an Italian mortgage.

3. Search and visits

A shortlist built from your brief, a first look by video, then a planned viewing trip. After each visit, tell us what worked and what didn't: the brief gets sharper with every no.

4. Checks before you commit

Before the offer, or at the latest before the preliminary contract, have the documents checked: land registry data and floor plans, registered mortgages and charges, planning permits, the energy certificate and, for apartments, the condominium position. For older and rural buildings, add a technical survey. The due diligence guide lists what to ask for.

5. The purchase offer

In Italy you usually make a written offer (proposta d'acquisto), signed by you, often with a deposit held by the agent. It states the price, the payment schedule, the dates for the preliminary contract and the deed, and any conditions, such as obtaining a mortgage. Once the seller accepts it and you're informed of the acceptance, it is binding on both sides.

If an agent brought the parties together, they're entitled to their commission from each party once the deal is concluded (article 1755 of the Civil Code), and an accepted offer can be enough. Ask how much and when before you sign.

6. The preliminary contract

The preliminary contract (contratto preliminare, or compromesso) sets out everything the deed will contain: price and payments, the deed date, what's included, the state of the property and the seller's guarantees that there are no charges and that the permits are in order.

  • The deposit (caparra confirmatoria). If you withdraw, the seller keeps it; if the seller withdraws, they owe you twice the amount (article 1385 of the Civil Code). The amount is agreed between the parties.
  • Registration. The preliminary contract must be registered with the Agenzia delle Entrate within 20 days of signing (30 if it's a notarial deed). The tax is €200, plus 0.5% of the deposit and 3% of any advance payments on the price, credited against the final registration tax when the deed is subject to it.
  • Extra protection. If the preliminary contract is signed before a notary, it can be recorded in the property registers (trascrizione), which protects you against a later sale or mortgage by the seller.

7. Money and mortgage

If you need a mortgage, the bank's valuation and approval must fit between the preliminary contract and the deed. If you're converting from another currency, this is when the amount and date are known and you can fix the rate. Payments must be traceable: bank transfers or banker's drafts, never cash.

8. The deed

On the day of the deed (rogito), the notary reads the deed to the parties; if you don't understand Italian, an interpreter translates it. You sign, the balance is paid, the notary collects the taxes and their fee, and you get the keys. The notary then registers the deed and updates the property registers and the cadastre. The notary guide explains the day in detail.

9. After the deed

Transfer the utilities into your name, register for the waste tax (TARI), note the IMU deadlines if it's not your main home and, if you're moving, register your residence. See utilities and after-sale services.

How long it takes

The time between the accepted offer and the deed is agreed between the parties. A simple cash purchase can close within weeks; a mortgage, documents to regularise or a seller who must first buy elsewhere all add time. Agree on dates you can keep, because missing them can cost you the deposit.

--- faq Q: Can I withdraw after signing an offer? A: Before the seller accepts it, within the terms the offer itself sets, usually yes. Once it's accepted and you're informed, withdrawing normally means losing the deposit and may expose you to further claims. Q: What's the difference between the compromesso and the rogito? A: The compromesso (preliminary contract) is a binding promise to buy and sell, with a deposit. The rogito is the deed before the notary that actually transfers ownership. Q: Who pays the estate agent? A: In Italy an agent who brings about the sale is entitled to a commission from each party, as agreed in writing. Ask before you sign the offer how much it is and when it's due. Q: Can two people who aren't married buy together? A: Yes. You can buy as co-owners in the shares you choose, and the deed states the shares. Think about what happens to each share on death, and take advice if you come from different countries.
